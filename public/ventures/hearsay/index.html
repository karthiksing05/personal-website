Technical report · NSA HEARSAY Audio Authentication Challenge · HackGT 13 · September 2026

Interpretable Synthetic-Speech Detection with Concept Formation and Diffusion Prototypes

Team SideQuests

Abstract

We describe a synthetic-speech detector and an explanation layer that makes its decisions open to inspection. The detector averages three fine-tuned self-supervised speech models. We trained it on data from which we removed format shortcuts and to which we added 11,900 copy-synthesis fakes. It scores a minimum detection cost (minDCF) of 0.0317 on the organizers' test set and 0.0274 [0.0231, 0.0306] on 27,779 held-out In-the-Wild recordings.

The explanation layer organizes the detector's representation into a COBWEB concept hierarchy and explains each clip with diffusion prototypes named by those concepts. It agrees with the detector's decisions at κ = 0.985. We study all 5,013 prototypes that explain the 1,671 test clips. They fall into eight families, five synthetic and three real, each tied to a class of synthesis system or of real speech. Every synthetic family matches a measurable acoustic profile. Relative to real speech, its clips show higher estimated SNR, lower long-term spectral ripple and more variable noise floors. Family-specific markers sit on top of that profile, such as a smooth LPC residual for diffusion TTS. Twenty-one case studies show how prototypes separate synthesis systems, keep degraded real speech real, and single out clips the detector may have missed.

1Introduction

Zero-shot voice cloning now requires only seconds of reference audio. An analyst who receives a suspicious recording needs two answers: how likely the recording is to be synthetic, and on what evidence. The challenge organizers made the second answer explicit. They asked teams to state what worked, to explain why, and to minimize black-box detectors.

Detectors built on large self-supervised speech models generalize better than earlier hand-crafted systems [Tak et al. 2022a; Ge et al. 2025]. They remain opaque, and they readily learn properties of a dataset instead of properties of synthesis [Müller et al. 2021]. Our detector follows this line and adds three safeguards: shortcut removal, controlled synthetic data and a calibrated ensemble. The larger part of this report concerns inspection. We ask what the detector's representation encodes and whether a person can read it.

We answer with two tools from the study of categorization. COBWEB [Fisher 1987] builds a concept hierarchy incrementally, as a model of how people form categories. Diffusion prototypes, obtained by test-time concept discovery (TTCG) [Wang et al. 2026b], describe an individual clip as a composition of a few modes of the learned distribution. We name each prototype by the COBWEB concept that contains it, so every explanation refers to training audio a person can listen to.

1.1 Contributions

  1. A shortcut-free detector. An audit shows that file timestamps alone separate the provided classes. A canonical view removes such cues, and copy-synthesis fakes teach the synthesis step itself. The calibrated ensemble reaches 0.0274 minDCF on 27,779 held-out recordings (§3).
  2. A concept space with a held-out basic level. We fit a COBWEB hierarchy on the detector's own representation. We select each clip's basic level by pointwise mutual information on held-out data, which peaks at an interior depth where the closed-form criterion does not (§4).
  3. Prototype inspection. We implement TTCG for this space and name its prototypes by concepts (§5).
  4. Prototype families and their acoustic criteria. We group all 5,013 test-set prototypes into eight families and measure what acoustically distinguishes the audio each family explains (§6). Twenty-one case studies examine individual decisions (§7).
  5. Evaluated explanations. We measure fidelity, faithfulness, stability, label-free structure and three cognitive capabilities against baselines (§8).

2Background

2.1 Task and metric

The system receives an audio file of at least two seconds of English speech and outputs P(synthetic). The organizers rank systems with the ASVspoof 5 detection cost function at a spoof prior π = 0.3, Cmiss = 1 and Cfa = 4 [Wang et al. 2024]. The package treats bona fide speech as the target class, so its false-alarm cost applies to a synthetic clip that passes as real:

\[ \mathrm{DCF}(\tau) = \mathrm{FPR}(\tau) + \frac{C_{\mathrm{fa}}\,\pi}{C_{\mathrm{miss}}\,(1-\pi)}\,\mathrm{FNR}(\tau) = \mathrm{FPR}(\tau) + 1.714\,\mathrm{FNR}(\tau), \]

where FPR is the share of real clips flagged and FNR the share of synthetic clips passed. minDCF is the minimum over τ. After calibration at the evaluation prior, the Bayes-optimal rule flags a clip when its expected cost of acceptance exceeds that of rejection:

\[ C_{\mathrm{fa}}\,P(\text{spoof}\mid x) > C_{\mathrm{miss}}\,P(\text{bona fide}\mid x) \;\Longleftrightarrow\; P(\text{spoof}\mid x) > 0.2. \]

2.2 Prototypes, exemplars and the basic level

People classify new instances by their resemblance to a category's central tendency, its prototype. Members close to the prototype are judged more typical and are learned faster [Posner and Keele 1968; Rosch and Mervis 1975]. Exemplar theories store the instances themselves and classify by similarity to them [Medin and Schaffer 1978; Nosofsky 1986]. Categories also form hierarchies, and people favour one level of such a hierarchy, the basic level, at which categories are most informative [Rosch et al. 1976]. Prototype-based networks carry these ideas into machine learning and explain a decision by pointing at learned prototypes [Snell et al. 2017; Chen et al. 2019].

2.3 COBWEB

COBWEB [Fisher 1987] sorts each new instance down a concept hierarchy. At every node it chooses among four operators: add the instance to the best child, create a new child, merge the two best children, or split the best child. It keeps the operator that maximizes category utility [Gluck and Corter 1985], the gain in predictability of attribute values that a partition affords:

\[ CU(\{C_1,\dots,C_K\}) = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K} P(C_k)\sum_i\sum_j\Big[P(A_i = V_{ij}\mid C_k)^2 - P(A_i = V_{ij})^2\Big]. \]

For continuous attributes, each concept stores a diagonal Gaussian, and utility rewards children that are tighter than their parent [Gennari et al. 1989]. We use the continuous implementation of the Teachable AI Lab (cobweb-private). When labels are supplied, its objective adds a label-entropy term; §4.5 removes that term to test its influence. We measure how informative a concept c is for an instance x by pointwise mutual information with the root, and define the basic level of x as the node on its path where this quantity, estimated on held-out data, peaks [Wang et al. 2026a]:

\[ \mathrm{pmi}(x;c) = \log p(x\mid c) - \log p(x\mid \text{root}), \qquad c^{*}(x) = \arg\max_{c\,\in\,\mathrm{path}(x)} \widehat{\mathrm{pmi}}_{\text{held-out}}(c). \]

2.4 Diffusion models as concept formation

A denoising diffusion model [Ho et al. 2020] learns to reverse a gradual noising process. With \(\bar\alpha_t\) the cumulative signal level at step t,

\[ x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon, \qquad \hat x_0(x_t) = \frac{x_t - \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\hat\varepsilon_\theta(x_t,t)}{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}}, \]

where the second expression is Tweedie's posterior mean [Efron 2011]. Wang, Singaravadivelan and MacLellan [Wang et al. 2026a] argue that the noised marginals of such a model describe a hierarchy of Gaussian prototypes, with high noise levels corresponding to general concepts and low noise levels to specific ones. Test-time concept discovery and composition (TTCG) [Wang et al. 2026b] uses this view to explain a single input: it climbs the score function from a noised copy of the input to nearby modes, selects a few of them and composes them. §5 describes our implementation, and §5.4 tests the noise-level hypothesis in our space.

3Detection system

3.1 Data

The organizers supplied DiffSSD [Bhagtani et al. 2024], about 70,000 synthetic clips from ten systems, and 242 real clips resampled from LJSpeech. We added 19,575 real recordings: LJSpeech (13,100), the ten LibriSpeech speakers that DiffSSD clones (1,152) and 80 further LibriSpeech speakers (5,323). We generated 11,900 copy-synthesis fakes by re-synthesizing training-split real recordings with two HiFi-GAN models, DiffWave and Vocos [Wang and Yamagishi 2023]. Each such fake shares its speaker and content with a real recording. The In-the-Wild dataset [Müller et al. 2022], 31,779 web recordings of real and synthetic speech by public figures, serves only for evaluation.

An audit preceded training. Byte hashes, decoded-audio hashes and acoustic landmark matching against 89,817 reference recordings found no test audio in any training corpus. The audit also found strong shortcuts in DiffSSD: file-system timestamps alone separate the classes (AUC 1.0), and sample rate, codec, level, leading silence and duration differ by class.

3.2 Canonical view and augmentation

Every clip passes through one canonical view: decoding to 16 kHz mono, trimming of edge silence, a crop to a test-like duration during training, a 7 kHz low-pass filter, DC removal, peak normalization and one-LSB dither. During training, 60% of clips also pass through one or two channel operations: lossy codecs, telephony, band-limiting, resampling, spectral tilt, noise, mains hum, reverberation or clipping. Both classes receive the same operations, so a degraded real recording remains a real example.

3.3 Detectors, fusion and calibration

We fine-tune three AntiDeepfake models [Ge et al. 2025] at a learning rate of 2 × 10−6 for three epochs, with fixed sampling shares per data family. Each produces a synthetic logit ℓk. We z-normalize the logits, average them and apply Platt scaling at the evaluation prior [Platt 1999]:

\[ s(x) = \frac{1}{3}\sum_{k=1}^{3} \frac{\ell_k(x) - \mu_k}{\sigma_k}, \qquad P(\text{synthetic} \mid x) = \sigma\big(a\,s(x) + b\big), \quad a = 6.84,\; b = -2.60. \]

A router re-scores uncertain clips (0.05 < P < 0.8) and clips longer than six seconds in two-second windows, and cross-checks lossy or band-limited files. It records every decision in a per-file trace.

4The concept space

4.1 Representation

The explanation layer reads the time-averaged final-layer representation of the XLS-R-2B detector, the input of its linear classification head. We standardize it and whiten it with principal component analysis to 32 dimensions, fitted on training clips. A linear read-out from these 32 dimensions reproduces the detector's logit with R² = 0.99997, so the space holds essentially all the information behind the decision.

4.2 Hierarchy

We fit a COBWEB hierarchy on 11,999 training clips, with source and channel as labels, and hold out 999 clips to estimate the basic level. Test clips enter the hierarchy by categorization alone: they are sorted down the tree without updating it.

4.3 The basic level

Figure 2 contrasts two criteria for the basic level. The expected pointwise mutual information computed in closed form under each concept's own Gaussian rises with depth at every level, because a narrower Gaussian always appears more informative about its own members. Under that criterion the basic level is a single-clip leaf. Estimated on held-out clips, pointwise mutual information rises to a peak at depth 7 and then falls. The median concept at that depth holds eight training clips. We use the held-out criterion throughout.

4.4 The concept atlas

Three properties of the atlas bear on interpretation. First, the root divides first by authenticity and by corpus: real LJSpeech (0.1), synthetic speech in the LJSpeech voice together with the commercial cloners (0.2), open cloners and voice conversion (0.3), and real LibriSpeech (0.4). Second, the commercial cloners ElevenLabs and PlayHT imitate LibriSpeech speakers, yet they sit under concept 0.2 beside diffusion TTS in the LJSpeech voice. The hierarchy therefore groups them by how they render audio rather than by whose voice they imitate. Third, channel conditions form their own sub-concepts on both sides of the authenticity split: reverberant real speech (0.1.2), noisy real speech (0.1.3, 0.4.3) and reverberant clones (0.3.3).

4.5 Structure without labels

Because the objective includes a label term, we refit the hierarchy on the same clips with no labels at all and named its concepts afterwards by their members. Table 3 reports purity by depth.

5Prototype inspection

Our explanation of a clip x has three parts, which we compute as follows.

5.1 Discovery

We train a denoising diffusion model on the 32-dimensional concept space. For each clip we noise its representation to levels t ∈ {50, 75, …, 400} and climb the learned score function to a nearby mode x*. Tweedie's formula maps each mode to a prototype mean m = x*/√ᾱt. The diagonal of the denoiser's Jacobian, estimated with Hutchinson probes [Hutchinson 1989], gives its covariance.

5.2 Selection and composition

We select up to three prototypes by greedy maximization of a facility-location objective over the 32 dimensions r. The standard normal root distribution provides the baseline br that a prototype must improve on. The objective is monotone and submodular, so greedy selection comes within a factor 1 − 1/e of the optimum [Nemhauser et al. 1978]:

\[ F(S) = \sum_{r=1}^{32} \max\Big(b_r,\; \max_{j\in S} \log \mathcal{N}\big(z_r;\, m_{jr},\, \Sigma_{jr}\big)\Big). \]

Each dimension is then assigned to the prototypes by a softmax over their log-likelihoods, and a prototype's share is the fraction of dimensions it accounts for. The composed explanation is a product of experts [Hinton 2002].

5.3 Naming and scoring

Each prototype mean is categorized into the COBWEB hierarchy. The concept it reaches names the prototype and supplies its sources, channels, synthetic share pfake(c) and most typical training clips. The explanation's own synthetic probability weights the concepts by share:

\[ P_{\text{proto}}(\text{synthetic}\mid x) = \sum_{j\in S} \text{share}_j \cdot p_{\text{fake}}(c_j). \]

This quantity is reported beside the detector's probability in every case study. Agreement between the two supports the decision, and a large gap marks the clip for review (§7.5).

5.4 Noise level and generality

The hypothesis of §2.4 predicts that prototypes found at high noise levels are named by larger, shallower concepts. Table 4 tests it on the 5,013 test-set prototypes.

6Prototype families and their criteria

6.1 Assignment

We assign each prototype to a family by the dominant source of the concept that names it. Thirteen sources yield eight families: two commercial cloners, open zero-shot cloners (XTTS v2, YourTTS), unit-based cloning and conversion (UnitSpeech, OpenVoice v2), diffusion TTS in the LJSpeech voice (DiffGAN-TTS, Grad-TTS, ProDiff, WaveGrad 2), and three real-speech families. A clip's dominant family is the family with the largest summed prototype share. Its purity is that share.

6.2 Acoustic criteria

To state what each family captures in measurable terms, we compare the test clips a synthetic family dominates (purity ≥ 0.66) with the 978 clips the real LibriSpeech families dominate. We use 20 interpretable features from the signal-processing track: prosody, background noise, spectral shape, the LPC residual and phase. The effect size is Cliff's δ, the probability that a family clip exceeds a real-attributed clip minus the reverse, with a 300-sample bootstrap interval. No test labels exist, so the comparison describes the audio each family explains rather than ground truth.

6.3 Findings

A shared synthetic profile. All five synthetic families differ from real-attributed clips in the same direction on five features: higher estimated SNR (δ = +0.48 to +0.93), lower long-term spectral ripple, more variable noise floors, flatter high bands and a narrower spread of spectral flux. All 25 of these family-by-feature differences have intervals that exclude zero. The prototypes therefore encode a common property of the synthesized audio in this data: it is cleaner and spectrally smoother than the recordings it imitates, and its noise floor varies more.

Family-specific criteria. Diffusion TTS in the LJSpeech voice is the most extreme case. Its clips are almost noiseless (median SNR 38.3 dB, δ = +0.93), have a smooth LPC residual (kurtosis 2.2 against 4.5, δ = −0.78), carry less energy below 250 Hz and contain more pauses. PlayHT also shows a smooth LPC residual (δ = −0.48), a mark of vocoder excitation. The open cloners and the conversion systems show flatter background noise (δ = +0.31 and +0.42). The real LJSpeech family differs little from real LibriSpeech: only frame-loudness variability and LPC residual kurtosis differ clearly, on ten clips.

A caution. A cleaner signal is not in itself evidence of synthesis. Studio recordings are also clean, and a detector that relied on cleanliness alone would flag them. The detector's out-of-domain accuracy (§3) and the channel case studies (§7.4) indicate that it relies on more than cleanliness. The stress view in §9, however, shows that simulated room acoustics still degrade it.

8.2 Faithfulness and stability

We test faithfulness by deletion [Samek et al. 2017] on the 66 of 3,000 explanations that mix synthetic and real concepts. Deleting the dimensions the explanation assigns to synthetic concepts lowers the detector's logit more than deleting those assigned to real concepts: by 0.79 SD [0.67, 0.89], in 92% of cases. Random dimensions give effects of 0.20 [0.14, 0.24] and 0.18 [0.13, 0.22] SD. Across three insertion orders the hierarchy changes (partition ARI 0.03–0.04), but the leading source of an explanation agrees for 94–95% of clips.

8.3 Failure localization

On 2,794 held-out and In-the-Wild clips with 73 errors, five concepts contain 27% of the errors. The largest are a 1,177-clip concept of XTTS v2 and YourTTS clones (14% error) and two small concepts of reverberant real speech (46% and 50% error). The concepts name the detector's weak conditions directly.

8.4 Cognitive capabilities

With a single example, COBWEB attributes 7% of the withheld generator's clips, against 0% for kNN and 1% for logistic regression. The two tie at three examples, and logistic regression leads from ten. Attribution of known generators stays at 0.87, so insertion causes no forgetting. For novelty, negative basic-level mutual information separates clips of an unseen generator with AUROC 0.87 ± 0.06, against 0.49 for a Mahalanobis distance. For error triage on In-the-Wild, ranking clips by disagreement between explanation and detector reaches an average precision of 0.38, nine times that of a random ranking (0.04). Combined with the detector's own uncertainty, it recovers 95% of errors in the top 10% of clips.

10Discussion and limitations

What the prototypes reveal. The prototypes attribute test clips to recognizable classes of synthesis. The attribution matches measurable acoustic differences, and it separates the synthesis system from the channel it passed through (cases 10, 13–16). The hierarchy groups systems by how they render audio rather than by whose voice they imitate (§4.4, case 11). This is what distinguishes the layer from a post-hoc saliency map: each explanation refers to concepts formed from, and exemplified by, real training audio.

What the prototypes cannot establish. No test labels exist. The acoustic criteria describe the audio each family explains, and the disagreement cases (Table 6) are candidates for review, not confirmed errors. The shared synthetic profile includes cleanliness, which also characterizes studio recordings. The detector's out-of-domain accuracy argues against reliance on cleanliness alone, but the stress results show that room acoustics remain a weakness.

Method limitations. The shipped hierarchy is fitted with labels; the label-free analysis bounds but does not remove this dependence. The noise-level hypothesis of diffusion concept formation does not hold in our space (Table 4), and prototypes found at large search radii can name synthetic concepts for real clips (case 17). The explanation layer reads one detector (XLS-R-2B) of the three. The LJ-voice diffusion family dominates only 41 test clips and the real LJSpeech family ten, so their criteria rest on small samples.

11Conclusion and future work

A calibrated ensemble of fine-tuned speech models, trained on shortcut-free data with our own synthetic examples, reaches minDCF 0.0317 on the organizers' test set and 0.0274 on 27,779 held-out recordings. A concept-formation layer makes its decisions inspectable. It agrees with the detector at κ = 0.985 and explains each clip through prototypes named by learned concepts. Across the test set, the prototypes form eight families with measurable acoustic criteria, and 21 case studies show how they separate synthesis systems from channels and single out clips for review.

  • Room acoustics. Training with measured room impulse responses addresses the clearest weakness, found by both the stress view and the explanation layer.
  • Radius-aware prototypes. Restricting or down-weighting high-noise prototypes would remove the spurious synthetic attributions of case 17.
  • Explaining the full ensemble. Concept spaces for the XLS-R-1B and MMS-1B members would let explanations cover every component of the score.
  • Newer generators. Open-weight systems released in 2025–26 would test whether the synthetic profile of §6.3 holds.
