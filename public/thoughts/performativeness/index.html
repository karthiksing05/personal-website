the world is not just a stage

2026-11-27Karthik Singaravadivelan

Over the past few years, I’ve watched a lot of the people around me (and, at times, myself) put real effort into looking like they care about something: boycotts announced on social media, effective altruism pledges, and LinkedIn posts about the newest club or hackathon. I think a lot of this starts much earlier than we give it credit for, in college applications and scholarship cycles that reward students for knowing what they want to do long before most of them actually do. In this post, I want to take a stance on what performativeness is actually worth. In short, I think performativeness can be a useful device for inspiration when it is well-read and used sparingly, but its overuse, along with selective ignorance, distracts from what actually makes a difference: direct involvement in the thing you care about, and keeping up to date with the sauce.

Well done is better than well said. - Benjamin Franklin, American polymath and Founding Father

definitions#

  • Performativeness: doing something for the signal it sends rather than for the effect it has. Signaling isn’t inherently bad, since it’s how you find people who care about the same things; the problem starts when the signal becomes the main reason for doing something.
  • Selective ignorance: choosing not to learn enough about an issue to feel responsible for it. I’d describe it as lackluster performativeness.
  • Well-read performativeness: speaking up about something only after doing the work to understand it.

where it starts#

In my opinion, a lot of performativeness is learned through college applications and scholarship cycles. This notion of knowing what you want to do is an unfortunate expectation, because very few students actually know yet, and the ones who get rewarded are often the ones with the most convincing story rather than the ones who have explored the most. Students learn to pick activities for how they will look, and by the time they reach the workforce, that habit has been reinforced for years.

It shows up later in a few familiar forms. Performative boycotting is abstaining from something (a brand, a platform, a technology) to signal disapproval, and for some, it is a mental game: it gives you the feeling of having taken a moral stand, at only the cost you’re willing to pay. Effective altruism, the philosophy of using evidence and reason to do the most good possible (often by earning a lot and donating it), is an idea I respect at its core, but I think a lot of EA culture has become about doing good without ever being directly involved in the causes being funded. LinkedIn farming and larp culture is the most obvious one: the post about how grateful you are to announce the thing, the club you joined for your resume, the hackathon you attended for the photo. The common thread, in my opinion, is that each of these optimizes for the appearance of involvement rather than involvement itself.

Selective ignorance is the other side of the same problem. In an experiment by Dana, Weber, and Kuang (2007), about 74% of participants chose the fair option when they could see how their choice affected someone else, but when the consequences were hidden behind a button they could press for free, 44% chose not to press it, and fair choices dropped to about 37%.

what each gets wrong#

AI is a good example of where a blanket take falls short. I don’t think boycotting AI is necessarily bad, and I understand a lot of the concerns behind it, but it is one of the most timely technologies of our generation, there are real pros to using it, and its direction is being decided right now by the people building, using, and regulating it. I’m confident a happy medium can be found, and finding it takes reason, judgement, and precision rather than a blanket take in either direction.

With effective altruism, the math of doing the most good is sound in principle, but when you aren’t involved in the cause yourself, it’s much harder to know whether the good is actually happening; the collapse of FTX in 2022 showed what can happen when nobody checks. LinkedIn larping is harmless to everyone else, but I’d argue it harms the person doing it, because it teaches you to value how something looks over what it does.

I’m not against being performative in every case, though. The problem, in my opinion, is that too often, being performative comes with loose information and loose engagement regarding the very thing you’re speaking up against. I’m absolutely not above being performative myself: my clearest example is that I’ll sometimes exaggerate my sense of a scenario, purely to show people the importance of deep and intentional research into their thoughts. Performativeness can be a device for inspiration in cases like this, but overuse of it detracts from its meaning. Even then, it is absolutely true that I research and update myself with the most relevant information before making decisions about my opinions, which is what I mean by well-read performativeness.

Related to this, it has become “trendy” to have hot takes, even where hot takes seldom need to be had. When everyone feels obligated to have a stance on everything, the result is an overcrowded space of unintelligible thoughts. It’s completely okay to say you don’t know enough about something yet.

direct involvement#

My personal thesis is that if you want to make a difference, there is no better way to do so than to be directly involved with the space you want to make the difference in. To me, direct involvement comes in three forms:

  • Money: have a lot of it, and donate it to the cause.
  • Policy: influence the rules that regulate the cause.
  • Building: make something so incredibly successful that people have no choice but to respect your take on the cause.

Direct involvement need not mean working in the career yourself; it can mean finding allies who are, or civic engagement, or just staying up to date with the sauce on the issues you claim to care about. To students in college and people new to the workforce specifically: in my opinion, picking a job where you “have fun” or “are useful” is a partial truth. True fulfillment (as I’ve written about before) comes from working on issues you feel good about, or from being involved with the people who are.

For me, that issue is energy. I believe the energy crisis is the biggest and most immediate crisis we face today, and also the space with the most room to make a difference, because of the large amount of compute humanity has newly unlocked: AI is driving energy demand up quickly, while also enabling new progress in materials discovery, grid optimization, and fusion research. It’s actually on my list of priorities to closely befriend someone working on experimental research into more sustainable forms of energy, so if that’s you, please reach out!

q.e.d.#

Most of us are taught performativeness early, through applications that ask us to know what we want before we’ve had the chance to find out, and by the time we’re in college or the workforce, it has become second nature. I don’t think the answer is to stop being performative entirely. Performativeness can still be a useful device for inspiration, as long as it’s well-read and used sparingly; it’s the overuse, the loose information, and the hot takes nobody needed that detract from its meaning, and selective ignorance is no better, since choosing not to look is just a quieter way of choosing not to act.

If you care deeply about something, the best place to get involved is on the thing itself, not through acts of abstinence, effective altruism, or performative blogposting (which, I’m aware, is a little ironic to write in a blog post). Stay informed, keep up to date with the sauce, find the people doing the work, and get directly involved however you can, whether that’s through money, policy, building, or simply befriending the people who are already doing it.

- Karthik
